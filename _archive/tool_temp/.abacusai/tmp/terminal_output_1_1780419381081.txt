1.​Executive Summary 
 
Flash floods are the deadliest natural hazard in Arizona, killing an average of 14 people per year and causing 
$180 million in annual property damage. The fundamental problem is not the flood — it is the time delay 
from storm forecasts to seeing where to warn people. Current physics-based flood simulation tools require 
4 to 6 hours to produce a flood inundation map. A monsoon flash flood in Southern Arizona can arrive in 
under 2 hours. That gap between warning and flood impact is where lives are lost. 
The Arizona Flash Flood Inundation AI Early Warning System (AFFI) closes this gap. AFFI is a 
six-staged physics-guided artificial intelligence pipeline that ingests real-time weather forecast data and 
delivers a probabilistic flood inundation map to authorized government personnel at their phone or 
Emergency Operations Centers (EOC) in about 60 seconds — 360 times faster than current methods — 
with accuracy comparable to physics-based models. Table ES-1 outlines the benefits of AFFI. 
This document describes the complete technical architecture of AFFI, piloted on the Upper Sonoita Creek 
Watershed (HUC 150503010204) in Patagonia, Santa Cruz County, Arizona. Every component of this system is 
designed for transferability: the same AFFI System, with watershed-specific data substitution, can be deployed 
for any watershed in Arizona or the United States. 
To improve model robustness and transferability, the AI system is first trained on USDA Walnut Gulch 
Experimental Watershed hydrologic and rainfall-runoff data and then fine-tuned for the targeted watershed 
deployment area. (Since the late 1950s the Walnut Gulch Watershed has long-term hydrology, erosion, and 
water quality data collection and research programs near Tombstone, Az.) The transfer-learning approach 
allows AFFI to leverage high-quality historical flood dynamics from a well-instrumented watershed while 
adapting to the terrain, hydrology, and storm-response characteristics of the specific target watershed. 
The full development of the AFFI system is broken into tasks, each with specific, verifiable deliverables. All data 
sources are publicly available from federal agencies at no licensing cost. The flood plain modeling software, 
Hec-Ras, is publicly available. Task 1 has already been completed and validated.

2.​Problem Statement: Flash Flooding in Rural Southern Arizona 
 
2.1​Human Cost 
Between 2000 and 2023, flash floods killed 287 people in Arizona — more than tornadoes, hurricanes, 
and earthquakes combined in the same period (NOAA Storm Data, 2023). Most fatalities occurred in 
vehicles, where drivers encountered flooded roadways with little or no advance warning. 
The pilot area for AFFI is the Upper Sonoita Creek watershed near Patagonia in Santa Cruz County, Arizona 
(HUC 150503010204). This drains 55.4 mi2 (143.6 km²) of semi-arid terrain and has experienced 12 major 
flood events since 1980, with peak flows exceeding the 50-year return period on three occasions. State 
Route 82 near Patagonia — the only evacuation route for 3,200 people — has been rendered impassable by 
flooding on multiple occasions with little or no advance warning to emergency managers. 
Example: The 2006 Santa Cruz County Monsoon Flood: A single monsoon event caused $12 million in 
infrastructure damage, displaced 340 residents, and closed State Route 82 for 72 hours. Emergency managers 
received no advance flood inundation map. Response was entirely reactive. This event is the baseline 
scenario AFFI is designed to mitigate. 
2.2​Infrastructure and Economic Cost 
The economic cost of flash flooding in Arizona averages $180 million annually in direct damage to roads, 
bridges, utilities, and structures (FEMA, 2022). Indirect costs include emergency response mobilization 
($2,400/hour for a county EOC), post-flood debris removal ($180,000–$450,000 per major event), 
long-term infrastructure repair ($1.2M–$8M per bridge or road segment), and agricultural losses 
($15M–$40M per severe monsoon season in Southern Arizona). 
2.3​The Technology Gap 
Southern Arizona's rural flash flood problem is uniquely diﬃcult. The region is characterized by hundreds of 
ephemeral washes and arroyos — channels that are dry for 300+ days per year but can carry catastrophic 
flows within minutes of drenching by a monsoon rain cell. These channels are ungauged, ephemeral, braided, 
and peak with a rapid response to rainfall. The time of concentration as short as 15–30 minutes for small 
sub-watersheds. 
Current flood mapping operational tools — NOAA's National Water Model, FEMA Flood Insurance Rate 
Maps, Google Flood Hub — were designed for large, gauged river systems. They provide valuable 
national-scale flood information but do not address the specific challenge of rural, ungauged, ephemeral

NOAA National Water Model (NWM): The NWM is the outstanding national-scale streamflow forecasting

Rainfall Forecast → Mean Areal Watershed Precipitation → Runoﬀ / Discharge → Flood Depth 
& Extent → Probability Maps → Alert 
Each arrow represents a distinct physical transformation. Each transformation is handled by the most

been proven to match or exceed physics-based accuracy at a fraction of the computational cost.

4.3​Stage 1: Meteorological Interface — Explained 
What it does: Ingests ensemble rainfall forecasts from NOAA's Global Forecast System (GFS, 31 members, 6- 
hourly updates) and the High-Resolution Rapid Refresh (HREF -High-Resolution Ensemble Forecast, ~10 
members, hourly, CONUS). For each watershed, computes Mean Areal Precipitation (MAP) is the spatially 
averaged rainfall over the watershed extent — for forecast horizons of 1, 3, 6, 12, 24, 48, 72, and 168 hours. 
Why it matters: A point rainfall measurement tells you how much rain fell at one location. A watershed 
responds to the average rainfall over its entire area. MAP is the physically correct input to a hydrologic 
model. Using point rainfall instead of MAP is a common error that leads to significant over- or 
under-prediction of flood response. 
What is AI at this stage? Stage 1 is NOT AI. It is physics-based numerical weather prediction (NWP) from 
NOAA's operational models. The AI begins in Stage 2. This distinction is important for scientific integrity and 
regulatory acceptance. 
Comparison to 10-year storm rainfall: Each MAP value is compared against NOAA Atlas 14 precipitation 
frequency benchmarks for Santa Cruz County, producing a return-period classification (2-year through 
100-year) and an initial alert level (Advisory / Watch / Warning). This allows emergency managers to 
immediately understand whether an incoming storm is a routine event or a rare, high-consequence event. 
4.4​Stage 2: Physics-Guided LSTM Hydrology Model — Explained 
What it does: Converts the MAP time series into a predicted streamflow hydrograph (discharge over time, in 
ft³/s) using a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) neural network trained on Walnut Gulch 70-year anchor 
dataset, fine-tuned on pilot watershed. 
Why LSTM? Rainfall-runoﬀ is a time-dependent process — today's soil moisture depends on yesterday's 
rainfall. LSTM networks are specifically designed to learn these temporal dependencies. They have been 
independently validated on 531 US watersheds by Kratzert et al. (2019), achieving Nash-Sutcliﬀe Eﬃciency 
(NSE) > 0.80 — matching or exceeding calibrated physics-based models. 
HOW: LSTM is trained in two stages: base training on a high-quality anchor watershed (Walnut Gulch), 
followed by fine-tuning on the pilot watershed. Reference transfer learning in hydrology (Kratzert et al. 2019 
already supports this) 
Why "physics-guided"? The LSTM incorporates static watershed attributes (drainage area, slope, soil 
permeability, vegetation index, elevation, aridity index) from the USGS GAGES-II dataset. These attributes 
encode the physical characteristics of the watershed into the model, enabling it to predict discharge for 
watersheds it has never seen — the key to generalizability, Model training follows a two-stage transfer learning 
strategy. In Stage 1, the LSTM is trained on the Walnut Gulch Experimental Watershed — the world's most

complete semi-arid flash flood dataset with 70+ years of record — learning fundamental desert hydrology: 
rapid infiltration, Hortonian overland flow, ephemeral channel routing, and monsoon precipitation-runoff 
dynamics. In Stage 2, the base model is fine-tuned on local gauge data for the target watershed (for the Sonoita 
Creek pilot) using only the final layers, requiring as few as 10–15 years of local data. This approach — base 
training on a data-rich anchor watershed followed by targeted fine-tuning — is established in the hydrology AI 
literature (Kratzert et al. 2019) and is the mechanism that enables generalization to ungauged watersheds. 
Comparison to 10-year storm peak runoff: The predicted peak discharge is compared against the 10-year, 
25-year, 50- year, and 100-year return period flows derived from USGS regional regression equations for 
Arizona. This tells emergency managers "how rare or how extreme” this event is. 
4.5​Stage 3: U-Net Hydraulic Surrogate Model — Explained 
What it does: Converts predicted discharge into a spatial flood inundation map (flood depth and extent raster) 
using a U-Net convolutional neural network trained on 250 physics-based HEC-RAS flood simulations. 
Why U-Net? U-Net was originally developed for medical image segmentation and is now the standard 
architecture for spatial prediction problems. It takes a 2D input (terrain + discharge) and produces a 2D output 
(flood depth map) — exactly the transformation needed for hydraulic modeling. 
Why train on HEC-RAS? HEC-RAS is the industry-standard open-source hydraulic model used by FEMA 
and USACE. By training the U-Net on HEC-RAS outputs, AFFI inherits the physical accuracy of HEC-RAS 
while operating 360× faster. The U-Net is a surrogate (emulator) of HEC-RAS, not a replacement. 
Physics constraints enforced: Three physics constraints are applied to the U-Net output: (1) Gravity 
constraint — water cannot flow uphill; (2) Monotonicity constraint — higher discharge must produce equal 
or greater flood inundation area and extent; (3) Mass conservation — total predicted runoﬀ volume cannot 
exceed total rainfall volume. These constraints ensure physical plausibility even for scenarios outside the 
training distribution. 
Comparison to 10-year to 100-year flood inundation: The predicted flood map is compared against 
pre-computed HEC-RAS flood maps for the 10-year, 25-year, 50-year, and 100-year period events. This provides 
emergency managers with a direct, intuitive benchmark: "This event is expected to produce flooding 
comparable to a 25-year storm."

4.7​Stage 5: Benchmarking - Explained 
What it does: Compares every AFFI-predicted rainfall, discharge, and flood map against established federal 
engineering benchmarks — NOAA Atlas 14 precipitation frequency data and USGS regional regression 
equations — to classify each event by return period (2-year through 100-year). This gives emergency managers 
an immediate, intuitive answer: "Is this a routine storm or a once-in-50-years event?" 
Why it matters: Emergency managers are trained to act on return period language. A raw discharge number 
(e.g., 4,200 ft³/s) is meaningless without context. Saying "this event is comparable to a 25-year storm" is 
immediately actionable. Benchmarking translates AI output into the engineering language that government 
decision-makers trust and are legally required to use. 
What is AI at this stage? Stage 5 is NOT AI. It is a deterministic comparison engine using published federal data. 
This distinction reinforces scientific integrity and regulatory credibility.

Task 1: Meteorological Forecast Interface 
Objective: Build and validate the system that ingests real-time weather forecast data and computes Mean 
Areal Precipitation (MAP) for the Upper Sonoita Creek Watershed, with alert threshold logic benchmarked 
against NOAA Atlas 14 return periods. 
Step-by-Step: 
•​Step 1.1 — Define Area of Interest (AOI): Load the Upper Sonoita Creek Watershed boundary 
(HUC 150503010204) from the USGS National Map. Define bounding box (N=31.85, S=31.47, 
E=−110.50, W=−110.90). This is the spatial domain for all precipitation calculations. 
•​Step 1.2 — Ingest GFS Ensemble Forecast: Connect to the Open-Meteo API (free, no key required) to 
retrieve 31-member GFS ensemble precipitation forecasts at 6-hourly intervals for 7-day lead times. 
Parse and store as structured arrays. 
•​Step 1.3 — Compute Mean Areal Precipitation (MAP): Spatially average the gridded GFS precipitation 
over the watershed boundary using area-weighted averaging. Compute MAP for accumulation windows 
of 1, 3, 6, 12, 24, 48, 72, and 168 hours. 
•​Step 1.4 — Load NOAA Atlas 14 Benchmarks: Load precipitation frequency estimates for Santa Cruz 
County, AZ from NOAA Atlas 14 Volume 1 for return periods of 2, 5, 10, 25, 50, and 100 years at all 
accumulation durations. These are the physical benchmarks against which all forecast rainfall is 
compared. 
•​Step 1.5 — Compute Return Period Classification: For each MAP value and accumulation duration, 
determine which return period bracket it falls into (e.g., "between 10-year and 25-year"). Compute a 
Storm Severity Index (SSI = MAP / MAP_10yr) for each duration. 
•​Step 1.6 — Apply Alert Threshold Logic: Apply the following thresholds calibrated for the Sonoita 
Creek watershed: Advisory (0.25"/1hr, 0.75"/24hr), Watch (0.50"/1hr, 1.25"/24hr), Warning (1.00"/1hr, 
2.00"/ 24hr). Generate an alert level (GREEN / ADVISORY / WATCH / WARNING) for each ensemble 
member and each lead time. 
•​Step 1.7 — Generate Alert Packet: Compile all outputs into a structured JSON alert packet 
containing: alert level, MAP values, return period classifications, ensemble statistics (mean, 10th, 50th, 
90th percentile), and time-to-peak estimate. 
•​Step 1.8 — Dummy Rainfall Stress Test: Validate the alert logic using synthetic rainfall scenarios 
spanning the full range from light shower to extreme cloudburst. Confirm that each scenario triggers 
the correct alert level and return period classification.

Task 2: LSTM training & transfer learning (Base Model → Fine‑tune) 
Objective: To teach AI how desert floods work. We start with a world-class foundational model (trained on 
Walnut Gulch Watershed) and "fine-tune" it to our specific pilot location at Sonoita Creek. This two-stage 
approach allows us to launch new watersheds in a fraction of the time and with far less local data. 
Step-by-Step: 
•​Step 2.1 — Data Setup: Collect historical rain and streamflow records from Walnut Gulch (our 
"Anchor" site) and Sonoita Creek (our "Pilot" site). 
•​Step 2.2 — Base Training: Train a "Foundation" AI model on Walnut Gulch’s 70-year record. This teaches 
the AI the general "physics" of Arizona flash floods. 
•​Step 2.3 — Benchmarking: Compare our AI's performance against historical physics models to ensure it 
is accurate and balanced. 
•​Step 2.4 — Local Fine-Tuning: Take the "Anchor" model and adapt it to Sonoita Creek. Instead of 
retraining the whole brain, we only update the parts that need to learn local soil and terrain. 
•​Step 2.5 — Event Testing: Test the final model against major historical floods (like the 2014 Hurricane 
Odile remnants) to verify the timing and size of alerts. 
•​Step 2.6 — Return Period Discharge Estimation: For each watershed, derive the 2yr, 10yr, 25yr, 50yr, 
and 100yr return period discharge values using USGS regional regression equations for Arizona. These 
become the hydraulic benchmarks for Task 3.

Task 3: U-Net Hydraulic Surrogate Model 
Objective: Train and validate a U-Net convolutional neural network that converts predicted discharge 
into spatial flood inundation maps, using HEC-RAS simulations as training data. 
Step-by-Step: 
•​Step 3.1 — Prepare Terrain Data: Download USGS 3DEP 1-meter lidar DEM for the Upper 
Sonoita Creek Watershed. The 25-watershed expansion is a activity (Task 7). In the U-Net is trained 
exclusively on the pilot watershed using HEC-RAS simulations specific to Sonoita Creek terrain. 
•​Step 3.2 — Build HEC-RAS Models: Construct a 2D HEC-RAS hydraulic model for the Upper Sonoita 
Creek Watershed using the conditioned 1-meter lidar DEM and Manning's roughness values from 
NLCD land cover. Calibrate against available historical high-water marks from the 2006 Santa Cruz 
County monsoon flood (the baseline event defined in Section 2.1) and the 2014 Odile remnant event. 
•​Step 3.3 — Run HEC-RAS Simulations: Execute 50 HEC-RAS simulations for the Upper Sonoita 
Creek Watershed (10 discharge levels: Q2, Q5, Q10, Q25, Q50, Q100, and 4 intermediate levels; 5 
replicates with varied Manning's n to capture roughness uncertainty). Expanding to 25 watersheds × 
250 simulations. Starting with 50 high-quality simulations on one watershed is sufficient to train the 
U-Net surrogate for  operational deployment. 
•​Step 3.4 — Design U-Net Architecture: Implement a ResUNet (U-Net with residual connections) with: 
input channels = DEM + slope + channel distance + discharge value; output channel = flood depth (feet); 4 
encoder/decoder levels; skip connections at each level. 
•​Step 3.5 — Train U-Net: Train on 50 simulations (1 watershed × 10 discharge levels + replicates), 
validate on 25 simulations (5 watersheds × 5 discharge levels), test on 25 simulations (5 held-out 
watersheds × 5 discharge levels). Use Adam optimizer, combined MSE + structural similarity loss. 
•​Step 3.6 — Enforce Physics Constraints: Post-process U-Net output to enforce: (1) gravity constraint 
— zero depth for pixels above predicted water surface elevation; (2) monotonicity — higher discharge = 
equal or greater extent; (3) mass conservation — total volume check. 
•​Step 3.7 — Validate Against HEC-RAS: Compare U-Net flood maps against HEC-RAS maps on held-out 
watersheds using Intersection over Union (IoU), Critical Success Index (CSI), and mean absolute depth 
error (MAE). Target: IoU > 0.75. 
•​Step 3.8 — Generate Return Period Flood Map Library: For each watershed, generate 
pre-computed flood maps for Q2, Q10, Q25, Q50, and Q100 using both HEC-RAS (reference) and U-Net 
(operational). These become the benchmark flood maps for comparison in the alert system.

Task 4: Probabilistic Risk Products 
Objective: Execute the full pipeline for all 31 GFS ensemble members and generate probabilistic flood risk 
products suitable for emergency management decision-making. 
Step-by-Step: 
•​Step 4.1 — Ensemble Pipeline Execution: Run the complete Task 1 → Task 2 → Task 3 pipeline 
independently for each of the 31 GFS ensemble members. This produces 31 flood depth maps for 
each forecast event. 
•​Step 4.2 — Compute Ensemble Statistics: For each pixel in the flood map domain, compute: median 
depth (50th percentile), 90th percentile depth (worst-case), probability of exceeding 0.5m depth, 
standard deviation (uncertainty), and ensemble agreement index. 
•​Step 4.3 — Generate Probability Map: Create a raster where each pixel value represents the 
probability (0–100%) that flood depth will exceed 0.5 meters. This is the primary decision-support 
product for emergency managers. 
•​Step 4.4 — Generate Uncertainty Map: Create a raster showing the standard deviation of predicted 
depth across 31 ensemble members. High uncertainty areas require more conservative decision-making. 
•​Step 4.5 — Compute Time-to-Peak: For each ensemble member, identify the time step at which 
peak discharge occurs at the watershed outlet. Report the ensemble mean and spread of time-to-peak 
as a lead time estimate for emergency managers. 
•​Step 4.6 — Generate EOC Dashboard: Compile all probabilistic products into a multi-panel 
dashboard suitable for display in a county Emergency Operations Center: median map, probability map, 
uncertainty map, ensemble hydrograph, alert timeline, and return period comparison.

1. Executive Summary

Flash floods are the deadliest natural hazard in Arizona, killing an average of 14 people per year and causing $180 million in annual property damage. The fundamental problem is not the flood itself — it is the time delay between a storm forecast and knowing where to warn people. Traditional physics-based flood simulation tools require four to six hours to produce a flood-inundation map. A monsoon flash flood in Southern Arizona can arrive in under two hours. That gap between warning and impact is where lives are lost.

The Arizona Flash Flood Inundation AI Early Warning System (AFFI) closes this gap. AFFI is a six-stage, physics-guided artificial-intelligence pipeline that ingests real-time weather-forecast data and delivers a probabilistic flood-inundation map to authorized government personnel — at their phone or Emergency Operations Center (EOC) — in about 60 seconds, roughly 360 times faster than current methods, with accuracy grounded in federal engineering benchmarks. Table ES-1 summarizes the benefits.

AFFI is best understood as a transferable platform, not a single-watershed demonstration. Its meteorological interface, hydrologic model, hydraulic flood library, probabilistic engine, benchmarking layer, alert logic, and operational dashboard are all watershed-agnostic by design; adding a new watershed is a data-substitution exercise, not a system rebuild (Section 8). To improve robustness and transferability, the hydrologic AI is first trained on the data-rich Babocomari River gauge record (a long-instrumented Southern Arizona basin) to learn the fundamentals of desert flash-flood behavior, then adapted to the specific target watershed.

All primary data sources are publicly available from federal agencies at no licensing cost, and the reference hydraulic-modeling software (HEC-RAS) is publicly available. The system is organized into six tasks, each with specific, verifiable deliverables (Section 5).

2. Problem Statement: Flash Flooding in Rural Southern Arizona

2.1 Human Cost

Between 2000 and 2023, flash floods killed 287 people in Arizona — more than tornadoes, hurricanes, and earthquakes combined over the same period (NOAA Storm Data, 2023). Most fatalities occurred in vehicles, where drivers encountered flooded roadways with little or no advance warning.

The pilot area for AFFI is the Upper Sonoita Creek watershed near Patagonia, in Santa Cruz County, Arizona (HUC 150503010204). It drains 55.4 mi² (143.6 km²) of semi-arid terrain and has experienced 12 major flood events since 1980, with peak flows exceeding the 50-year return period on three occasions. State Route 82 near Patagonia — the only evacuation route for 3,200 people — has been rendered impassable by flooding on multiple occasions with little or no advance warning to emergency managers.

Example — the 2006 Santa Cruz County Monsoon Flood: a single monsoon event caused $12 million in infrastructure damage, displaced 340 residents, and closed State Route 82 for 72 hours. Emergency managers received no advance flood-inundation map; the response was entirely reactive. This event is the baseline scenario AFFI is designed to mitigate.

2.2 Infrastructure and Economic Cost

The economic cost of flash flooding in Arizona averages $180 million annually in direct damage to roads, bridges, utilities, and structures (FEMA, 2022). Indirect costs include emergency-response mobilization (~$2,400/hour for a county EOC), post-flood debris removal ($180,000–$450,000 per major event), long-term infrastructure repair ($1.2M–$8M per bridge or road segment), and agricultural losses ($15M–$40M per severe monsoon season in Southern Arizona).

2.3 The Technology Gap

Southern Arizona's rural flash-flood problem is uniquely difficult. The region contains hundreds of ephemeral washes and arroyos — channels that are dry for 300-plus days per year but can carry catastrophic flows within minutes of a monsoon rain cell. These channels are ungauged, ephemeral, braided, and respond rapidly to rainfall, with times of concentration as short as 15–30 minutes for small sub-watersheds. Existing operational tools — NOAA's National Water Model, FEMA Flood Insurance Rate Maps, Google Flood Hub — were designed for large, gauged river systems. They provide valuable national-scale information but do not address real-time, event-specific inundation for rural, ungauged, ephemeral arroyos. No existing operational system provides real-time, event-specific flood-inundation maps for ungauged rural arroyos in Southern Arizona with lead times sufficient for emergency-management action. AFFI fills this gap.

2.4 The Climate Trend

Climate projections for the Southwest United States indicate a 15–40% increase in extreme-precipitation intensity during monsoon events by 2050 (IPCC AR6, 2021). Flood events that currently occur once every 50 years are projected to occur once every 20–30 years by mid-century. The window for investing in flood-inundation early-warning infrastructure — before these events become routine — is now.

4. Technical Solution: The AFFI Architecture

4.1 System Overview — The Physical Pipeline

AFFI follows the physically correct sequence for flood prediction — the same sequence by which rainfall actually becomes a flood. Each step is handled by the most appropriate method: physics-based numerical weather prediction where physics is well understood, artificial intelligence where it has been proven to match or exceed physics-based accuracy at a fraction of the computational cost, and established engineering procedure (SCS Curve Number, hydraulic geometry, federal design-storm benchmarks) where those give a fast, transparent, defensible result.

4.2 Why This Architecture and Not Others

This six-stage design is the only architecture that satisfies all five operational requirements simultaneously: speed (~60 s, required for flash-flood warning), accuracy (grounded in federal benchmarks, required for government trust), transferability (universal code, watershed-specific data, required for rural and statewide deployment), interpretability (each stage has physical meaning, required for regulatory acceptance), and uncertainty quantification (probabilistic output, required for risk-based decisions). A single end-to-end deep-learning model (rainfall directly to flood map) fails on transferability — it memorizes one watershed. A pure physics model fails on speed. The staged hybrid is the only design that passes all five tests.

4.3 Stage 1 — Meteorological Interface

What it does: ingests a 31-member ensemble rainfall forecast from NOAA's Global Forecast System and computes Mean Areal Precipitation (MAP) — the spatially averaged rainfall over the watershed — for horizons of 1, 3, 6, 12, 24, 48, 72, and 168 hours.

Why it matters: a single rain gauge tells you the rain at one point; a watershed responds to the average rain across its whole area. MAP is the physically correct input to a hydrologic model. Using point rainfall instead is a common error that leads to large over- or under-prediction.

Is this AI? No. Stage 1 is physics-based numerical weather prediction from NOAA's operational models plus deterministic area-weighted averaging. The AI begins in Stage 2. Each MAP value is also compared against NOAA Atlas 14 precipitation-frequency benchmarks for Santa Cruz County, producing an initial return-period classification and alert level so managers immediately know whether a storm is routine or rare.

4.4 Stage 2 — Hydrology Model

What it does: converts the rainfall history into a peak-discharge estimate — how much water will move through the channel. Desert hydrology has an unusual structure: the channel is dry roughly 95% of the time, and nearly everything that matters happens on a handful of days each year. Forcing one model to handle both regimes wastes most of its capacity learning "predict near-zero." AFFI instead uses a two-part hurdle model that mirrors this structure.

Why "physics-guided"? The models read hydrologically meaningful features — an antecedent-precipitation soil-moisture proxy, consecutive dry days, a monsoon-season flag, and 3- and 7-day rainfall accumulations — rather than raw numbers alone. And the system is trained in two stages, so a new watershed does not need decades of its own data:

Validated performance (held-out test, Upper Sonoita Creek). The model reports its own skill honestly through a technical-staff diagnostics panel:

MetricValuePlain-language meaning

The system is deliberately engineered so that decisions rest on the reliable signal — event detection (0.959) — while treating exact magnitude as a range and defaulting road-closure decisions to the worst-case scenario. This is described in the decision framework in Section 4.8.

4.5 Stage 3 — Hydraulic Flood Mapping

What it does: converts a discharge value into a spatial flood-depth map. AFFI's operational engine is a pre-computed, discharge-indexed flood-map library built entirely from federal open data — real FEMA regulatory floodplain geometry, real FEMA Base Flood Elevations (429 surveyed points for this watershed), and the real USGS 3DEP 10-metre elevation model. The principle a non-technical reader needs is simple:

The library holds eight maps spanning the 2-year through 500-year return periods (discharge from 83.6 to 620.5 m³/s). At runtime, today's predicted discharge is matched to the nearest stored maps and interpolated between them; discharges below the smallest map are scaled using an established hydraulic-geometry relationship (Leopold & Maddock, 1953). Why this method? Building a full 2-D hydrodynamic (HEC-RAS) model bank is 20–40 hours of calibrated modeling per watershed. An analytical library grounded in FEMA-effective floodplain geometry, published base flood elevations, and real lidar terrain delivers an operational, fully traceable, return-period-indexed depth map today. A neural hydraulic surrogate (a residual U-Net that emulates HEC-RAS) is in parallel development for full hydrodynamic fidelity; it reuses the same library interface, so it can be substituted with no change to the rest of the pipeline.

4.6 Stage 4 — Probabilistic Output

What it does: a forecast is never a single certain number, so AFFI propagates the optimistic (P10), most-likely (P50), and pessimistic (P90) rainfall percentiles — drawn from the 31-member ensemble — through the pipeline to produce three flood maps: best case, likely case, and worst case. Each percentile is converted to discharge using the SCS Curve-Number runoff method and looked up in the flood library.

4.7 Stage 5 — Benchmarking

What it does: compares every predicted rainfall, discharge, and flood map against federal engineering benchmarks — NOAA Atlas 14 precipitation frequency and USGS Log-Pearson III discharge statistics — to classify each event by return period (2-year through 500-year). Emergency managers are trained to act on return-period language: a raw discharge figure means little, but "comparable to a 25-year storm" is immediately actionable and is the same vocabulary FEMA maps already use. Like Stage 1, this stage is not AI — it is a deterministic comparison against published federal data, which reinforces scientific and regulatory credibility.

4.8 Stage 6 — Alert Policy and Decision Support

Critical policy decision: All AFFI alerts are delivered exclusively to authorized government personnel — county emergency managers, public-works directors, and EOC staff. No alert is broadcast directly to the public. This eliminates the liability of automated public warnings and ensures that all public communication is reviewed and authorized by responsible officials.

The delivered front end is a purpose-built dashboard that surfaces, from the shared forecast: an interactive map with per-building and per-facility flood depths and a flood-probability layer; a Decision Cockpit giving time-to-peak (Kirpich time-of-concentration → SCS lag), the life-safety probability (chance depth exceeds 0.5 m), and a plain-English confidence framework; an NWS-style bulletin generator; and a data-staleness watchdog that visibly warns if the forecast pipeline has not refreshed. The full pipeline re-runs automatically every six hours.

7. Validation Plan and Performance Goals

AFFI is validated against four independent benchmarks. The performance targets (Table 6) follow published standards in the peer-reviewed hydrology literature; the achieved pilot results are reported alongside them.

Table 6: Performance Goals and Achieved Results
Validation LevelMethodTargetAchieved (pilot)
Event detectionHeld-out flood/no-flood days—AUC-ROC 0.959 ✓
In-sample magnitudeHeld-out years, peak dischargeNSE > 0.75NSE 0.348 (see note)
Historical event / spatialFull pipeline vs. post-event surveysIoU > 0.75In progress
Speed benchmarkGFS input → flood map< 60 seconds~60 seconds ✓

Note on the magnitude metric. An NSE of 0.348 is below the 0.75 target, and this is reported plainly rather than hidden. It is a known property of the regime, not a defect in the implementation: NSE is normalized by the variance of observed flow, which makes it structurally harsh for arid, ephemeral basins where flow is near-zero most of the time and spikes violently on a few days. The large-sample hydrology literature consistently finds arid/ephemeral basins to be the lowest-NSE class of watershed across all model types (Kratzert et al., 2019). AFFI is therefore engineered so that decisions rest on the reliable signal — event detection (AUC-ROC 0.959) — with magnitude treated as a ±25–40% band and road-closure decisions defaulting to the worst-case scenario.

8. Transferability to Other Watersheds

Design principle: every component of AFFI was built to be transferable to any watershed in Arizona or the United States. Upper Sonoita Creek is the first validated deployment, not the product. The code of every stage is watershed-agnostic; only data changes per watershed.

9. From Forecast to Action: Concept of Operations

A forecast only saves lives if it reaches the right person and triggers the right action in time. AFFI is therefore designed to fit the internationally recognized structure for effective early warning — the four pillars of the World Meteorological Organization's Early Warnings for All framework. A warning system that is strong in one pillar but weak in another does not protect anyone; the table below shows how AFFI contributes to all four.

AFFI is a decision-support tool for authorized officials — it informs a chain of human decisions, it does not replace them. As the alert level rises, the recommended posture and the responsible roles escalate with it:

10. Limitations and Honest Constraints

A tool used for life-safety decisions must be as clear about what it cannot do as about what it can. The following constraints are stated plainly so that no one over-trusts the system in a situation it was not built for.

  • Flood size is a band, not a precise number. The model is strong at detecting whether a flood will occur (event-detection AUC-ROC ≈ 0.96) but less precise about exactly how big it will be, which is inherently hard in flashy desert streams. Magnitude should be read as a range, and worst-case (P90) values should drive life-safety decisions.
  • One watershed validated to date. Upper Sonoita Creek is the first validated deployment. The architecture is built to transfer, but results in a new basin should be re-validated, not assumed.
  • Spatial accuracy not yet field-validated. Formal comparison of predicted flood extents against post-event ground surveys (an IoU study) remains the primary open validation activity; the flood maps are anchored to FEMA-effective floodplain geometry in the meantime.
  • The hydraulic library is an analytical simplification. Depth maps are built from FEMA base flood elevations and lidar terrain rather than full 2-D hydrodynamic (HEC-RAS) simulation — a deliberate, defensible choice for the pilot, with a neural surrogate in parallel development for higher fidelity.
  • Confidence falls with lead time. A 5–7 day outlook is for planning and pre-positioning; road-closure and evacuation decisions belong to the 0–24 hour window when confidence is highest.
  • Channel flooding, not every puddle. AFFI forecasts flooding along the mapped creek/wash corridor. Very localized urban street ponding outside that corridor is not its focus.
  • Decision support, not authority. AFFI does not issue public warnings or make evacuation decisions for anyone. When AFFI and the National Weather Service disagree, the NWS is authoritative; in an immediate emergency, 911 and the county EOC govern.

11. Operations, Sustainability, and Governance

11.1 How It Stays Current — and Fails Safe

The full pipeline re-runs automatically every six hours and on demand, and the dashboard refreshes on its own during an event. Critically, the system is honest about its own health: a data-staleness watchdog visibly warns when the forecast has not refreshed on schedule, so a stalled pipeline can never masquerade as a calm, current "all-clear."

11.2 Cost and Sustainability

Every data source AFFI depends on is free and public from a federal agency, which removes licensing cost and a major category of long-term risk. A full forecast cycle costs on the order of pennies of cloud compute, and the built-in dashboard assistant runs locally at no per-use cost. Sustainability is a design property, not an afterthought.

11.3 Provenance, Security, and Reproducibility

Every number on the dashboard traces to a named federal source, and the interface deliberately keeps official government data (e.g. National Weather Service alerts) visually distinct from AFFI's own model output so the two are never confused. The government API is access-controlled with audit logging, and alerts are restricted to authorized personnel. The entire stack is containerized for one-command, reproducible deployment, which is also what makes transfer to a new watershed a matter of substituting data rather than rebuilding software.

Plain-language definitions of the terms used in this document and on the dashboard, for a first-time reader.

Appendix B — Model Card

A structured summary of the AI hydrology model, following the model-card standard for transparent reporting (Mitchell et al., 2019).

FieldDetail
ModelTwo-stage hurdle model: an LSTM binary event-gate (is today a flood day?) followed by an XGBoost peak-discharge regressor (how big?), with hydrologically-motivated input features.
Intended useDecision support for authorized emergency-management personnel: forecasting flood occurrence and approximate magnitude in the Upper Sonoita Creek watershed to inform road-closure, staging, and evacuation-planning decisions.
Primary usersCounty emergency managers, public-works directors, and EOC staff.
Out-of-scope usesPublic-facing automated warnings; issuing evacuation orders; individual "is my house/road safe" decisions; forecasting localized urban ponding outside the mapped channel; use in an un-validated watershed without re-validation.
Training dataAnchor: Babocomari River gauge record (USGS 09471000) for general desert flash-flood behavior. Adaptation: Sonoita Creek gauge record (USGS 09481500). Features derived from rainfall history (antecedent precipitation, dry-day counts, seasonal and multi-day accumulation signals).
EvaluationHeld-out test on Upper Sonoita Creek. Event detection AUC-ROC 0.959, AUC-PR 0.643, F1 0.611; magnitude NSE 0.348, PBIAS −2.9%.
Ethical considerationsA life-safety tool: designed to keep a responsible official in the loop (no automated public alerts), to be visually honest about model-vs-official-source, and to fail safe (staleness watchdog). Population-at-risk uses aggregate gridded data, not personal information.
Caveats & recommendationsTrust detection over exact magnitude; default life-safety decisions to the worst-case scenario; treat the National Weather Service as authoritative on disagreement; re-validate before relying on the model in a new basin.
